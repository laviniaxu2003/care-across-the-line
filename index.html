Four years after Dobbs

Care Across the Line

Potential access to abortion care depends on geography, on local healthcare resources, and on provider networks that cross state lines.

Claire Gutermuth · Chloe Qiu · Lavinia Xu · CS 1710 · Data through July 2026

Distance

How far would you have to travel for abortion care?

Suppose you lived in one of the states that now have the most restrictive abortion policies. In May 2022, the month before the Supreme Court’s Dobbs decision, the nearest open abortion clinic was on average about 40 road miles away. Eight months later it was more than five times as far.

These figures are averages for all counties in states classified as Most Restrictive, weighted by the number of women aged 15–44 living in each county. They describe how far away the nearest clinic is, not an estimate of any one person’s trip. In the Most Protective states, the same average stayed near nine miles: 9.7 in May 2022 and 8.9 in January 2023.

The increase was not evenly distributed.

Where distance grew

Three states account for most of the added distance burden

To compare places of very different sizes, we multiplied the number of women aged 15–44 in each county by the extra miles to the nearest clinic between May 2022 and July 2026, then added those amounts up by state. Counties where the nearest clinic got closer count as zero, so a shorter trip in one place does not cancel a longer one somewhere else.

Texas alone accounts for 69.5% of this added burden. Together with Louisiana (9.4%) and Tennessee (4.5%), three states account for 83.4%.

Distance is one barrier. Where the nearest clinic moved far away, local maternity care was often thin as well.

Local resources

Longer journeys overlap with limited local care

Of the 589 counties where the nearest clinic was at least 100 miles farther away in July 2026 than in May 2022, 316 had no OB-GYN recorded in 2023, and 297 of those 316 also had no hospital providing obstetric care. Most of the 316 counties (246) are nonmetro.

The OB-GYN and hospital counts are a single 2023 snapshot. They do not show that providers left after Dobbs. They show that large increases in distance often fell on places that already had little local maternity care.

States with restrictive policies have more rural counties, and rural counties tend to have fewer OB-GYNs. Comparing metro counties with metro counties, and nonmetro with nonmetro, shows whether the difference between policy groups is only a matter of rurality.

Local resources tell only part of the story. The nearest clinic may be across a state line.

Across state lines

Access does not stop at the state line

16.7 million women aged 15–44 live in counties whose nearest open abortion clinic is in another state.

That is 25.7% of the women aged 15–44 in the counties covered by the data. A state’s policy shapes where clinics operate inside its borders, but the nearest option for many residents is in a neighboring state, and those neighbors become the nearest option for people well beyond their own residents.

Clinics and catchments

A small number of clinics can be the nearest option for a large region

Kansas has 7 clinics. They are the nearest option for 6,128,642 women aged 15–44 who live in other states, plus 545,960 Kansas residents (of 563,769; the rest have a nearer clinic in another state). That is 6,674,602 women in all, or about 953,515 per clinic.

What follows

What this means for planning

These data describe where the nearest clinic is and what other care exists nearby. They cannot identify an optimal way to fund or place services, but they point to four priorities.

  • 01Look beyond state policy maps to understand cross-border access. For 16.7 million women aged 15–44, the nearest clinic is in another state.
  • 02Prioritize communities with long journeys and limited local healthcare resources. 316 counties gained at least 100 miles and had no OB-GYN in 2023; most are nonmetro.
  • 03Consider potential regional catchments when allocating clinic resources and travel support. Kansas’s 7 clinics are the nearest option for 6.1 million women in other states.
  • 04Use geographic access data alongside service-capacity and utilization data for more equitable planning. Distance and clinic counts show potential access only; they say nothing about appointment availability, staffing, or who actually travels.
