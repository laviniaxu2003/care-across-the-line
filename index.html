Care across
the line

Abortion bans are written one state at a time. They are not felt one state at a time.

Claire Gutermuth · Chloe Qiu · Lavinia Xu · CS 1710

The hook

Seven clinics in Kansas are the nearest option for 6.1 million women who don’t live there.

Count the clinics providing abortion in the United States and almost nothing has happened. There were 790 in May 2022, the month before Dobbs came down. There are about the same number today.

Count them state by state and the floor gives way. 13 states now have none at all. The clinics did not disappear. The demand they served didn’t either. It crossed a state line.

What changed · 1

The drive got long, and only in some places

In the states with the strictest bans, the average woman’s drive to the nearest clinic went from 37.5 miles in May 2022 to 215.6 miles by January 2023. In the most protective states it never left nine. In Hinds County, Mississippi, home to the clinic at the centre of Dobbs itself, the drive went from six and a half miles to three hundred and forty.

But this did not happen everywhere. Only 41% of US counties saw their drive change at all, and Texas, Louisiana and Tennessee alone account for 83% of the total added burden. This is not a national drift. It is a handful of corridors.

What changed · 2

It landed where care was already thinnest

Of the 589 counties where the drive grew by a hundred miles or more, 316 had no OB-GYN at all, and 297 of those had no hospital offering obstetric care either. Across the two most restrictive policy tiers, 54.5% of counties have no OB-GYN, against 30.2% in the most protective.

The easy explanation is that those states are simply more rural. So we adjusted for exactly that, giving the restrictive states the same mix of city and country as the protective ones. Even then, 64 to 89% of the gap is still standing.

What changed · 3

The map nobody draws: who serves whom

Almost every map of this subject colours a state by its own law. That hides the thing that actually happened. This one colours states by where their women now have to go, and it exposes service territories that pay no attention to state lines at all.

What it means

A state can ban abortion inside its own borders. It cannot keep the consequences inside them.

Sixteen point seven million women now have their nearest clinic in a state they do not live in, do not vote in, and cannot hold to account. That is a quarter of everyone of reproductive age in America. The capacity to serve them was never built, because no legislature was ever responsible for building it.

What follows

Where the pressure actually needs relieving

  • 01Capacity has to be planned regionally. Kansas licenses and funds clinics for 564,000 residents while standing as the nearest option for 6.7 million women. No single state legislature is answerable for that gap, which is exactly why it keeps growing.
  • 02The pressure is concentrated enough to act on. Three states carry 83% of the added burden and ten counties carry 47% of it. Relief aimed at a few corridors would reach most of the people affected.
  • 03Outcomes have not moved yet, and that is the argument for moving now. The infant mortality gap between the most and least restrictive states was 1.60 per 1,000 before Dobbs and 1.77 after: wide, steady, and already there before any ban was signed. The access shock is new. The outcome shock, if it comes, has not arrived.
