Data & methods

Where every number comes from

The site is built from processed tables the team assembled from public sources. Some values in those tables are observed counts, some are modeled estimates made by the source, and some are measures we derived. This page says which is which, and what the numbers cannot tell you.

The sources

Five public sources, merged by county FIPS code into the team’s processed tables (October 2026). The charts on this site use the first three; the CDC sources are kept in the repository for possible future views.

What is derived by us: distance change shares, the added-distance burden, the 316-of-589 flag, the state-to-state catchment tables, per-clinic catchments, the metro/nonmetro shares and every headline number. They are computed by scripts/build_data.py from the supplied tables and checked by scripts/validate.py. The supplied tables themselves were built by an earlier team script (build_final.py) from raw downloads that are not in this repository, so the steps before those tables cannot be rerun from here.

Definitions and formulas

  • AAverage distance by policy group. The mean of county road miles to the nearest open clinic, weighted by women aged 15–44, taken from the supplied access_trend_by_tier.csv (not re-averaged). The hook compares May 2022 with January 2023: 37.5 → 215.6 miles for Most Restrictive states.
  • BAdded distance burden. For each county, women aged 15–44 × max(miles in July 2026 − miles in May 2022, 0), summed by state and divided by the national sum. The unit is population × additional nearest-clinic miles, a geographic exposure measure. Counting reductions as negative values would give Texas, Louisiana and Tennessee 86.2% instead of 83.4%.
  • CDistance change and local resources. The county change compares May 2022 with July 2026, a different comparison from the hook. “At least 100 more miles and no OB-GYN” is a flag we derived from that change. It is not the supplied hit_twice column, which uses current distance (July 2026 ≥ 100 miles), and the supplied access_band also describes current distance, so neither is used for change. Of the 297 counties with neither an OB-GYN nor an obstetric hospital, 230 are nonmetro.
  • DShortage by policy group. Share of counties with OB-GYN data that recorded none in 2023: counts of counties, not population-weighted. Ranks 1–2: 54.5% of 1,637; ranks 6–7: 30.2% of 653. (Rank 1 alone is 52.8%; the site always uses ranks 1–2.) As a single check on rurality, giving ranks 1–2 the metro/nonmetro mix of ranks 6–7 (43.3% metro) gives 51.8% versus 30.2%, leaving about 88.8% of the crude gap. An earlier draft quoted “64–89% remains”; we could not reproduce the other specifications behind that range, so the site shows the metro/nonmetro comparison instead. Neither is a causal adjustment.
  • ECatchments. Each county is assigned to the state holding its nearest open clinic in July 2026. For each receiving jurisdiction: retained = residents assigned to their own state; incoming = residents of other states assigned to it; total assigned = retained + incoming; per clinic = total assigned ÷ open clinics. Outgoing population is kept separately. Every county with a known destination and population is counted exactly once (65,158,822 women). The earlier version added a state’s whole resident population to its incoming catchment, which counted residents whose nearest clinic is in another state; for Kansas that overstated the total by 17,809.
  • FReference line. The “50 states combined” line divides the population assigned to the 50 states by their 798 clinics: 81,573 per clinic. DC is left out of both because its clinic count is not in the supplied data. The earlier “81,655 national average” mixed the national population with the 50-state clinic count, and the story no longer uses a ratio to it.

What these numbers do not say

  • 01Distance is exposure, not travel. We measure how far the nearest clinic is, not where anyone actually goes. Telehealth, shield laws, gestational limits, appointment availability, money, time off work and childcare all change real journeys, and none of them appear here.
  • 02Catchments are potential, not workload. A catchment is the population whose nearest clinic is in a state. It is not a count of patients, appointments or abortion seekers, and clinic counts say nothing about staffing or capacity. The population is women aged 15–44, the group the source data report, which is not everyone who may need abortion care.
  • 03Policy is a fixed snapshot. Every month and year is labeled with Guttmacher’s October 2026 classification. The trend chart therefore shows how places now classified as restrictive changed, not when each law took effect.
  • 04Local resources are a 2023 snapshot. OB-GYN and obstetric-hospital counts were measured once, in 2023. They are not a pre-Dobbs baseline and do not show providers leaving.
  • 05Descriptive, not causal. Overlaps between distance, local resources, rurality and policy are descriptions. They do not isolate the effect of any policy.
  • 06Health outcomes are not part of this story. The supplied infant mortality, preterm and prenatal-visit tables cover only a few post-Dobbs years and are aggregated by state. They cannot show either harm or the absence of harm, so the story does not use them.

Reproducing the site

From the repository root: pip install -r requirements.txt, then python3 scripts/build_data.py to rebuild data/derived/ and run all validation checks, then python3 -m http.server and open http://localhost:8000. The page must be served over HTTP; D3, TopoJSON and the fonts load from public CDNs, so it also needs an internet connection. Details are in README.md and docs/.
